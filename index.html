What DIIMED is building

DIIMED is an education and advocacy initiative focused on dyslexia (and frequently associated learning differences) in medical training and practice. The goal is a system where competence is measured by clinical ability, and where supports are structured, consistent, and stigma-free. Competence is clinical ability—not conformity to broken systems.

Clinical Overview

Definition (clinical language)

Dyslexia is a heterogeneous group of language-based learning disorders characterized by difficulty with reading and spelling despite adequate intelligence, educational opportunity, and motivation.

It is typically associated with difficulties in accurate and fluent word recognition and poor spelling and decoding abilities—often stemming from a deficit in the phonological component of language that is unexpected relative to other cognitive abilities and the provision of effective instruction.

Secondary consequences: reduced reading experience can impair vocabulary growth, background knowledge accumulation, reading comprehension, and confidence over time.

Clinical reality in medicine: Dyslexia can be invisible in high-achieving learners until high-volume reading environments (board exams, dense documentation workflows, time-pressured chart review) expose the mismatch between ability and speed-based measurement.
Dyslexia Phenotypes

Common patterns (not one-size-fits-all)

Phonological dyslexia: impaired phonological awareness and decoding; difficulty sounding out unfamiliar words.

Surface/orthographic dyslexia: difficulty with visual word form recognition; irregular words are harder; slower sight vocabulary acquisition.

Double-deficit dyslexia: combined phonological impairment plus rapid automatized naming/fluency weakness; often more persistent functional impact.

Key cognitive processing characteristics

Phonological awareness: manipulating sounds (segmenting, blending).

Rapid naming and fluency: speed of accessing labels for familiar stimuli; affects reading rate.

Working memory: holding and manipulating verbal information; impacts multi-step reading/writing tasks.

Processing speed: time pressure vulnerability (tests, dense note-writing workflows).

Orthographic mapping: building durable word representations through repeated exposure.

Across the Lifespan

Functional impact (school → training → practice)

An uneven profile of abilities is common: above-average strengths (visual-spatial reasoning, pattern recognition, design/diagram thinking) may coexist with impaired decoding, spelling, and phonological processing.

In medicine, dyslexia may affect standardized exam performance, time-pressured chart review, documentation speed, and high-volume reading demands—without reflecting a lack of clinical competence.

Protective factors: early identification, structured support, evidence-based instruction, mentorship, and safe disclosure culture.

Frameworks

Neurobiological & theoretical frameworks

Phonological deficit theory: dyslexia commonly involves impaired phonological processing that disrupts decoding and word recognition.

Sensory processing hypotheses: atypical visual or auditory processing may contribute in some individuals (heterogeneous mechanisms).

Overlap with ADHD: attention and executive-function demands can compound reading/fluency vulnerability; comorbid ADHD is common and can worsen functional outcomes if untreated.

Epidemiology & Risk

Prevalence, genetic/familial risk, and prognostic factors

Prevalence: commonly cited estimates place dyslexia at approximately 5–10% of the population, with variability by language, orthography, and identification practices.

Family history: familial clustering is common; parental literacy skills and family reading difficulty history are strong predictors of offspring risk.

Sex differences: identification is often higher in boys, but referral/diagnostic bias and differing classroom behaviors can influence observed ratios.

Risk and protective factors (expanded)

Risk: early language delay, family history, comorbid ADHD or language disorder, and limited access to evidence-based instruction.

Protective: early screening, structured phonics-based interventions when indicated, coaching on workflow strategies, accommodations that reduce speed-based penalty, and stable social support.

Neurodevelopmental Comorbidity

ADHD overlap and mental health associations

Neurodevelopmental overlap is common: ADHD, developmental language disorder, dyscalculia, and other learning differences may co-occur with dyslexia.

ADHD + dyslexia often predicts worse functional and mental health outcomes than dyslexia alone when supports are delayed or inconsistent.

Mental health: elevated stress, depression/anxiety risk, and burnout vulnerability can emerge when barriers persist and the environment is punitive.

High-risk signal: Increased risk of suicidal ideation and suicide attempts has been reported across children, adolescents, and adults with learning disabilities in adverse contexts.
Protective: high social/emotional support and timely accommodations predict better mental health outcomes.
Strengths

Dyslexic strengths can benefit medical practice

Visual-spatial reasoning: strengths in visual thinking and spatial reasoning can benefit radiology, surgery, procedural medicine, anatomy, and systems navigation.

Pattern recognition: big-picture, systems-based thinking can support integrative diagnosis and longitudinal problem framing.

Creativity and innovation: lateral problem-solving can produce novel approaches to clinical workflow, patient education, and research design.

Why This Matters

Inclusion is a workforce strategy and a patient safety strategy

Medicine depends on diversity of cognition, not uniformity of performance on speed-based reading tasks. When dyslexic trainees are supported by structured systems, patients benefit through retention of skilled clinicians with unique strengths.

Bottom line: inclusion is not charity—it is competence preservation.

Education

Tailoring training for individual needs

Training should recognize different cognitive profiles and provide different strategies to reach the same competency outcomes.

Evidence-based accommodations and structured supports allow performance to reflect true clinical capability rather than speed-based reading constraints.

Practical training design targets

Workflow engineering: templates, checklists, and tools that reduce cognitive load without reducing clinical accuracy.

Assessment alignment: measure clinical reasoning and patient care outcomes, not raw reading speed.

Coaching: structured coaching on chart review, prioritization, and documentation strategy.

Inclusion

Creating an inclusive environment

Inclusive environments reduce stigma and improve help-seeking and safe disclosure.

Policies should normalize accommodations, support proactive coaching, and prevent retaliation or informal penalties for disability disclosure.

Support

Collaboration and support

Faculty development, peer mentorship, and structured coaching improve learning efficiency and reduce burnout risk.

Teams perform best when strengths are leveraged and documentation workflow is engineered for safety and speed without sacrificing accuracy.

Systems

Administrative responsibility in medical education

Administrative systems should reduce unnecessary friction and ensure timely accommodations, transparent processes, and consistent communication.

Systems must prioritize patient safety and learner safety and must not rely on informal negotiation to access basic disability supports.

Systemic Barriers

Barriers inside medical training systems

Medical training environments often reward speed and volume in reading-based tasks rather than safe clinical reasoning and competence.

Stigma, inconsistent accommodation processes, unclear standards, and weak feedback systems can punish disclosure and delay support.

Goal: reduce friction, clarify processes, and prevent disability from becoming an invisible tax on trainees.

USMLE & Licensing Barriers

Accommodation denial is a progression barrier

In a national survey (Petersen et al., PLOS ONE, 2022) involving 141 schools, among students who applied for Step 1 accommodations: 52% were denied, 51% experienced delayed progression, and 76% took Step 1 unaccommodated. Among those who tested unaccommodated, 32% failed.

Primary source confirmation (verbatim summary)

PLOS ONE national survey (Petersen et al., 2022) reports 52% denied, 51% delayed progression, 76% took Step 1 unaccommodated, and 32% failed among those unaccommodated.

Interpretation: denial/delay is not a small inconvenience; it can directly alter progression, finances, morale, and workforce retention.
Advocacy

Advocating for change

Advocacy requires consistent institutional pathways, faculty education, transparent accountability, and measurable processes.

DIIMED supports systems change, education, and support structures that improve retention of dyslexic learners and clinicians.

Legal grounding and policy frameworks

U.S. Department of Justice (ADA): law, regulations, and standards (ADA.gov).

ACGME Equity Matters: GME inclusion framework and learning environment resources.

AAMC: disability inclusion reports and learner support guidance.

Accountability

Institutional accountability toward learners with disabilities

Institutions must ensure timely responses, clear documentation, consistent standards, and safe escalation pathways.

Accountability requires feedback loops, leadership ownership, and visible metrics of inclusion outcomes (retention, time-to-accommodation, denial rates, and safety culture indicators).

Testing and Assessment

Accurate diagnosis and documentation guide effective accommodations and appropriate supports. Psychoeducational or neuropsychological assessment may be necessary depending on training stage, institutional requirements, and functional impact.

Principle: assessments should clarify functional needs for accommodations and instructional strategy.

Assessment resources

Assessment resources vary by region and training context. Common domains include reading accuracy, decoding, spelling, fluency, processing speed, and working memory.

Inclusion frameworks

Community support matters. Mentorship, peer groups, and professional networks reduce isolation, normalize lived experience, and provide practical guidance for navigating training and licensure.

Practical models: physician mentorship pairing, learner-to-learner peer groups, and institutional disability champions to reduce administrative drift and delays.

Services and Planned Services

Participation and capabilities

DIIMED supports the development of inclusion-focused services, including workshops, institutional consultation, workflow mapping, and mentorship network development. These services are designed to align evaluation with real clinical competence.

Participation: educators, administrators, physicians, and medical students can collaborate on policy clarity, time-to-accommodation metrics, and sustainable support pathways.

How to get involved

Educators: faculty development and inclusion training, policy review, and process design.

Learners: mentorship pathways, resource navigation, and structured workflow strategies.

Institutions: accountability metrics, time-to-accommodation improvements, and safe disclosure systems.

Conclusion

Call to action

Physicians with disabilities inform healthcare practices, enrich learning environments, and may reduce disparate population health outcomes. Research indicates that physicians with disability are more likely to provide care for underserved populations. DIIMED calls for educators, administrators, physicians, and medical students to foster inclusivity and support throughout medical education and practice.

Dyslexia inclusion extends beyond meeting quotas or academic standards. It is about recognizing and harnessing the full potential of individuals with dyslexia—who are entirely capable of excelling in medicine. By embracing these strengths, we enhance diversity of thought and approach within the profession and improve the overall practice of medicine.
